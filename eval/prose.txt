The town of Fairhaven sat at the mouth of a slow river, where fishing boats returned each evening with their holds half full and their crews full of opinions. For two hundred years the harbor had shaped every habit of the place: the bakery opened before dawn because the boats left before dawn, the school term bent around the herring season, and the church bell rang twice on foggy mornings so the pilots could take a bearing off the sound.

When the new bridge was announced, the town split in the quiet way small towns do. The council praised the jobs it would bring and the tourists who would no longer need the ferry. The fishermen pointed out that the pylons would sit directly across the channel where the tide ran strongest, and that no engineer who had ever hauled a net in a crosswind had signed the plans. Letters were written. Meetings were held in the hall with the bad radiator, and people who had not raised their voices in years discovered they still could.

Marta Ellison, who kept the ferry, said the least and lost the most. Her grandfather had carried mail across the river in a rowboat; her mother had run the first diesel launch. Marta knew the channel the way other people know a staircase in the dark, and she knew that bridges do not simply cross water — they change what the water means. A river you must cross slowly is a neighbor. A river you pass over at sixty miles an hour is scenery.

The bridge opened in October, two years late and well over budget, with a ribbon and a band and a speech from a minister who mispronounced the town's name. The ferry ran for one more winter, mostly empty, mostly out of stubbornness. In the spring Marta sold the launch to a birdwatching outfit up the coast and took a job at the harbormaster's office, where she answered radios and told strangers, with great patience, where the shallow water was.

What surprised everyone was how little the town changed, and then how much. The bakery still opened before dawn, but for commuters now instead of crews. The school no longer bent its term around the herring, because the herring, for reasons no one could prove and everyone suspected, had moved offshore. On foggy mornings the church bell still rang twice, though nobody steered by it anymore, and the sound carried over the bridge to people driving somewhere else, who heard it and thought, without slowing down, that it was a lovely old custom.
